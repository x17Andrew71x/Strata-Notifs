# Relic Vault fossil catalogue research

Status: approved for production on 2026-10-10. The official catalogue uses the 17 fossils below.

Research date: 2026-10-09

## Purpose

Replace the three invented Relic Vault fossils with a broad, recognisable and semi-realistic fossil catalogue. The visual reward should come from authentic anatomy, preservation quality and mineral character—not from assigning different rarity labels to the same picture.

The approved first catalogue contains exactly:

- 10 common fossils
- 5 uncommon fossils
- 2 rare fossils

## What rarity means here

This is a game-facing preservation tier, not a price guide and not a claim that every occurrence of a taxon has the same rarity.

- **Common** means the depicted hard part or preservation form is genuinely abundant or routinely represented in the fossil record.
- **Uncommon** means the organism may be familiar, but the depicted completeness is substantially harder to preserve: articulated skeletons, connected limbs, intact whole bodies or amber entrapment.
- **Rare** means either the preservation is explicitly described as extremely uncommon or the named fossil is known from a very small number of specimens.
- Local geology matters. A fossil common in one formation can be absent elsewhere; these tiers are deliberately broad rather than pretending to be global occurrence statistics.
- The catalogue should never assign two rarities to one unchanged asset. Rarity belongs to the catalogue item and its fixed paired artwork.

## Approved catalogue

### Common — 10

#### 1. Dactylioceras-like ammonite shell

- **Scientific anchor:** Ammonoidea; a ribbed Jurassic ammonite form rather than a living nautilus.
- **Evidence:** Ammonite fossils occur around the world, sometimes in very large concentrations, and their shells are abundant in the fossil record.[1]
- **Why common:** The durable coiled shell is the ordinary collectible form; soft anatomy is not shown.
- **Museum cue:** One complete ribbed spiral in dark limestone, cream calcite in the chambers, restrained bronze label.
- **Excavation cue:** The identical shell partly embedded in compact brown-grey marine sediment, with ribs crossing several dig tiles.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** No octopus arms, no nautilus hood and no exposed soft tissue.

#### 2. Belemnite rostrum

- **Scientific anchor:** Belemnitida; the bullet-shaped calcitic guard or rostrum.
- **Evidence:** Belemnites lived through the Jurassic and Cretaceous and are commonly found as fossils.[2]
- **Why common:** The hard rostrum is the recognisable fossil part; a whole soft-bodied animal would imply exceptional preservation and is not this item.
- **Museum cue:** A small cluster of three aligned guards, honey-calcite fractures, no squid body.
- **Excavation cue:** The same three guards angled through fine sediment like pale stone darts.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Guards should be tapered and solid-looking, not teeth, claws or shells.

#### 3. Spiriferid brachiopod shell

- **Scientific anchor:** Brachiopoda; a winged, strongly ribbed spiriferid-style shell.
- **Evidence:** Brachiopods were particularly abundant in the Palaeozoic and are often the most common fossils in rocks of that age.[3]
- **Why common:** A single durable shell is representative of their abundant fossil record.
- **Museum cue:** A broad bilateral shell with a central fold and radiating ribs in matte grey calcite.
- **Excavation cue:** The same shell exposed along one valve surface in shallow seafloor mudstone.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Brachiopod symmetry runs through each valve; do not render it as an ordinary left-right bivalve shell.

#### 4. Gryphaea oyster valve

- **Scientific anchor:** *Gryphaea arcuata*, the Jurassic oyster popularly called a devil’s toenail.
- **Evidence:** Bivalve valves are the parts usually preserved; the BGS identifies the recurved *Gryphaea* form and notes its Jurassic occurrence.[4]
- **Why common:** This item is one robust recurved valve, not the much less usual pair still joined at the hinge.
- **Museum cue:** One heavy hooked oyster valve with layered growth bands and worn umbo.
- **Excavation cue:** The same valve resting convex-side-up in soft dark sediment.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Keep the asymmetrical bowl and recurved beak; it must not resemble an ammonite spiral.

#### 5. Crinoid stem columnals

- **Scientific anchor:** Crinoidea; separated or short connected stem ossicles.
- **Evidence:** Crinoid soft tissue decays and complete specimens are rare, but stem parts are common fossils.[6]
- **Why common:** The item deliberately depicts the common stem material rather than a complete crown.
- **Museum cue:** A short articulated stem beside several loose star-holed columnals on black stone.
- **Excavation cue:** The identical discs form a broken rosary through pale limestone chips.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Show central lumens and repeated calcite discs; do not make them vertebrae or coins.

#### 6. Rugose horn coral

- **Scientific anchor:** Rugosa; a solitary horn coral from the Palaeozoic.
- **Evidence:** Rugose corals are among the coral groups most frequently found as fossils and are commonly seen in Silurian-to-Carboniferous rocks.[7]
- **Why common:** A solitary calcitic corallum is robust and widely recognisable.
- **Museum cue:** One tapering horn cut open at the calice to reveal radial septa.
- **Excavation cue:** The same horn lies diagonally in limestone with the calice partly clogged by sediment.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** The top needs a radial cup structure; avoid mammal-horn texture or living coral colour.

#### 7. Lamniform shark tooth

- **Scientific anchor:** A modest lamniform tooth; not a giant megalodon trophy.
- **Evidence:** Sharks can shed many thousands of teeth, and teeth are the most common part of a shark to fossilise.[8]
- **Why common:** Use a small, complete tooth with ordinary preservation rather than an enormous flawless apex-predator tooth.
- **Museum cue:** One triangular enamel crown with a darker mineralised root on a low mount.
- **Excavation cue:** The same tooth sits point-up in sandy marine sediment.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Keep a clear crown/root boundary and plausible modest size; no fantasy serrations.

#### 8. Carbonised fern frond

- **Scientific anchor:** A Carboniferous seed-fern-like frond preserved as a compression; the item remains taxonomically conservative rather than asserting a species from appearance alone.
- **Evidence:** Compression and carbonisation commonly preserve fine detail in leaves, and carbonisation is particularly effective for leaf material.[9]
- **Why common:** The item is an ordinary leaf compression, not preserved soft animal tissue.
- **Museum cue:** A branching black frond impression across a warm grey split shale slab.
- **Excavation cue:** The same slab surface lies flush with compact silt, its leaflets crossing the tile grid.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Flat carbon film and venation only; no green living foliage or raised cast-metal leaves.

#### 9. Domal stromatolite

- **Scientific anchor:** Layered microbial structure built by cyanobacterial communities.
- **Evidence:** Stromatolites are a major constituent of the late Archaean and Proterozoic fossil record, peaked in abundance roughly 1.2 billion years ago and fossilise as they form when their structures remain intact.[10]
- **Why common:** “Common” refers to their major presence in those ancient rock records, not to modern living stromatolite localities.
- **Museum cue:** A cut domal section with alternating cream, rust and charcoal laminae.
- **Excavation cue:** The same dome emerges as nested curved bands in shallow stone.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Layered sedimentary geometry, not tree rings, agate crystals or a generic striped pebble.

#### 10. Echinocorys-like echinoid test

- **Scientific anchor:** Echinoidea; a heart/oval sea-urchin test without attached soft tissue.
- **Evidence:** Fossil echinoids are common in Jurassic and Cretaceous rocks, especially Late Cretaceous Chalk; the test and spines are the parts normally fossilised.[12]
- **Why common:** Depict a robust test alone. Connected spines or internal feeding apparatus would represent more exceptional preservation.
- **Museum cue:** One pale oval test with five petaloid ambulacra and fine pore rows.
- **Excavation cue:** The identical test is half-set in chalky sediment.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Preserve fivefold symmetry and pore bands; do not render a modern spiny living urchin.

### Uncommon — 5

#### 11. Articulated trilobite

- **Scientific anchor:** A complete enrolled or prone trilobite, broadly *Calymene*/*Phacops*-like without copying a named museum specimen.
- **Evidence:** Trilobites and their moulted fragments can be common, but many fossils are discarded moults and partial cephalons or pygidia; limbs and antennae rarely preserve.[11]
- **Why uncommon:** The reward is a coherent articulated body with cephalon, thorax and pygidium together, not merely a common fragment or moult.
- **Museum cue:** Complete dorsal exoskeleton with crisp segmentation, muted pyrite flecks and no soft appendages.
- **Excavation cue:** The same articulated body lies in a split shale plane with fine sediment in the joints.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Three longitudinal lobes, repeated thoracic segments and a plausible head/tail distinction.

#### 12. Articulated fossil fish

- **Scientific anchor:** A small ray-finned fish preserved laterally on a laminated lakebed slab.
- **Evidence:** NPS taphonomy material notes that most fossil fish at Fossil Butte are partly disarticulated because decay can scatter their bones.[13]
- **Why uncommon:** This item keeps the skull, vertebral column, ribs and fins in life order.
- **Museum cue:** A complete warm-brown skeleton on pale laminated limestone, with no reconstructed missing half.
- **Excavation cue:** The same slab is partly covered by fine lake silt; the spine crosses the centre tiles.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Anatomical fish skeleton, not a white cartoon fish outline; no soft flesh.

#### 13. Complete starfish

- **Scientific anchor:** A fully articulated fossil asteroid on a marine slab.
- **Evidence:** Well-preserved fossil starfish are rare; fossils are typically partly or entirely disarticulated, and preservation of an entire animal is rare.[18]
- **Why uncommon:** The five arms remain connected and the ossicles remain in position, but no impossible colour or living tissue is shown.
- **Museum cue:** A complete five-armed calcitic outline with subtle ossicle detail on dark ash-grey stone.
- **Excavation cue:** The same extended star lies flat in fine sediment, with two arm tips initially concealed.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Small repeated ossicles and believable asymmetry; avoid a perfect decorative star icon.

#### 14. Articulated fossil crab

- **Scientific anchor:** A brachyuran crab with carapace and major limbs still associated.
- **Evidence:** More than 3,000 fossil brachyuran species are known, but delicate features are a different preservation class: fossil crab eyes are described as extremely rare.[14]
- **Why uncommon:** This is not merely a common isolated carapace. The reward keeps the carapace, claws and walking legs together; eyes remain mineral impressions rather than fleshy organs.
- **Museum cue:** Flattened ochre carapace with articulated claws and several legs, framed by a dark concretion.
- **Excavation cue:** The same crab lies in a rounded carbonate nodule with limbs radiating beneath sediment.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Five paired limb positions including claws, plausible joints and no modern cooked-crab redness.

#### 15. Insect in amber

- **Scientific anchor:** A small gnat or midge inclusion inside fossil resin.
- **Evidence:** Amber can preserve insects and other organic material, but this preservation route is described as quite rare.[15]
- **Why uncommon:** The amber itself is not the whole reward; a clearly legible insect inclusion is required.
- **Museum cue:** One irregular translucent honey-amber piece, backlit softly, with a small dark winged inclusion.
- **Excavation cue:** The identical amber piece is lodged in lignite-rich sediment, dusty rather than polished.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Tiny plausible insect, internal depth and resin flow lines; no oversized mosquito or dinosaur-DNA theatrics.

### Rare — 2

#### 16. Dinosaur embryo in egg

- **Scientific anchor:** A curled non-avian dinosaur embryo visible within a broken or naturally exposed fossil egg.
- **Evidence:** Embryonic dinosaur bones are explicitly described as extremely uncommon finds.[16]
- **Why rare:** The egg and diagnostic curled embryonic skeleton survive together; rarity comes from developmental preservation, not scale or glow.
- **Museum cue:** A stone egg opened along a natural fracture, revealing a compact curled skeleton in cream calcite; richer pedestal lighting than lower tiers.
- **Excavation cue:** The identical fractured egg rests in a nest-like sediment bowl, with shell fragments and embryo gradually exposed together.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Embryo must be inside an egg, compact and incomplete-looking where matrix obscures it; no fully formed miniature adult posed theatrically.

#### 17. Archaeopteryx slab

- **Scientific anchor:** *Archaeopteryx*, with articulated skeleton and feather impressions on fine limestone.
- **Evidence:** The Field Museum describes its specimen as one of only 13 on Earth and among the most complete and best-preserved found.[17]
- **Why rare:** The catalogue uses the documented scarcity of the taxon and its feather preservation. It is a fictional scientifically plausible specimen, not a copy of the Chicago slab or any other named original.
- **Museum cue:** A broad pale limestone slab with a dark articulated skeleton and a delicate feather halo; this should be the immediate “wow” item without magical effects.
- **Excavation cue:** The same slab lies in a fine-grained limestone bed; feather traces only become legible over the final few tiles.
- **Accuracy guardrails:** Long feathered forelimbs and tail, small toothed skull and fine impressions; never a modern bird silhouette, bat wing or glowing angel motif.

## Screened-out near misses

- **Turritellid gastropod:** gastropods became the most common molluscs after the Palaeogene,[5] but another coiled shell made the common set feel less varied beside ammonites, brachiopods and *Gryphaea*.
- **Complete crinoid crown:** scientifically strong as an uncommon preservation state, but it would duplicate the crinoid identity already represented by common stem columnals.
- **Megalodon tooth:** visually impressive but too easy to make every shark-tooth reward feel oversized; the initial common item should establish a more ordinary baseline.

## Distribution

The 10/5/2 catalogue is a content mix, not itself a probability table. Item weights remain individually tunable in `FossilCatalog.kt`.

The approved initial roll behaviour is:

- common tier: 86% total, with weight 86 for each of ten fossils
- uncommon tier: 12% total, with weight 24 for each of five fossils
- rare tier: 2% total, with weight 10 for each of two fossils
- duplicate protection may later reduce immediate repeats without letting users influence the pre-rolled daily result

This preserves a meaningful rare reveal while giving the ten-fossil common pool enough variety for daily use.

## Paired-art contract

Every approved item must be generated from one isolated fossil master and then composited into two fixed scenes:

1. **Excavation:** top-down, compact sediment bed, unpolished object, identical crop and camera geometry across the set.
2. **Museum:** frontal three-quarter display, black-stone pedestal, identical room, camera, lighting and label position across the set.

The fossil’s silhouette, breaks, mineral veins and orientation must match in both scenes. The excavation version may add dirt and reduce polish, but it cannot become a separately generated lookalike.

### Rarity must be visible without labels

- **Common:** one readable specimen with visibly adhered dry-earth clumps, grit or smears, ordinary matrix, faded matte calcite/iron colours and low ornament.
- **Uncommon:** greater anatomical completeness with a weathered, dusty, imperfect preparation that remains modestly clearer than common.
- **Rare:** exceptional anatomy or preservation, meticulous preparation and controlled museum-natural colour—but no oversaturation, fantasy aura, levitation, neon or invented gemstone body.

## Completed approval gates

- Andrew approved the 17-item roster and review-v4 visual treatment.
- Each image was checked against its scientific guardrails.
- Every excavation/Museum pair derives from one approved chroma master and the two fixed templates.
- Production images are 960×960 lossy WebP at quality 82/method 6, stripped of EXIF and content-addressed by SHA-256; measured fidelity exceeds the former quality-86 JPEG exports while total catalogue bytes are 20.5% lower.
- Legacy fictional IDs resolve to canonical fossils so unfinished digs and existing Museum records remain usable.
- IDs, weights, bundled assets, migration behaviour and catalogue tests changed only after approval.
